## History of the 201 Area Code

![New Jersey 201 Prefix History Timeline 1947 to 2001](/images/blog/201-image-4.webp)

The **201 area code** holds a unique distinction: it was the very first area code assigned to New Jersey, and it has served the state in some form since the NANP launched in **1947**. At that time, the 201 code covered the entire state — every city, suburb, and rural community in New Jersey.

For decades, area code 201 served all of New Jersey before being progressively refined through a series of splits as population and telephone demand surged. By the early 2000s, mobile device proliferation and new business line growth were pushing the remaining number supply toward exhaustion.

### The 1991 Split: 908 Created

On **June 8, 1991**, the southern and central regions of the state were carved out to form the **908 area code**, leaving 201 to cover northern New Jersey.

### The 1997 Split: 973 Created

On **June 1, 1997**, Essex County (home to Newark) and Passaic, Morris, and Sussex counties were reassigned to the new **973 area code**, reducing 201 to its current footprint in Hudson and Bergen counties.

### 2001: The 551 Overlay

By the late 1990s, the combined growth of mobile devices, fax machines, and new business lines was exhausting the remaining 201 number pool. Rather than splitting the territory — similar to the overlay approach used by the [315 area code](/blog/315-area-code) in Central New York — Verizon lobbied for an overlay instead.

The **551 area code** was introduced on **December 1, 2001**, as the first overlay in New Jersey's history. Both 201 and 551 serve the identical geographic territory of Hudson and Bergen counties. Existing 201 subscribers kept their numbers; new subscribers entering the market received 551 numbers.

A key consequence: **mandatory 10-digit dialing for all local calls**. Dialing just a 7-digit number no longer connects — every call, including those to a neighbor on the next block, requires the full area code prefix.

## 201 Area Code Time Zone

![10-Digit Dialing and Eastern Time Zone Guide](/images/blog/201-image-2.webp)

This region operates in the **Eastern Time Zone (ET)** — the same time zone as New York City:

- **Eastern Standard Time (EST):** UTC−5 — observed from early November to mid-March
- **Eastern Daylight Time (EDT):** UTC−4 — observed from mid-March to early November

### Cross-Timezone Calling Guide

**To/from the West Coast (PT):** Pacific Time is 3 hours behind Eastern. A 9 AM ET call reaches a Los Angeles contact at 6 AM PT — too early for business. Schedule West Coast calls for the 201 zone after noon ET (9 AM PT) for mutual business hours.

**To/from the Midwest (CT):** Central Time is 1 hour behind Eastern. A 10 AM CT call reaches the 201 zone at 11 AM ET — easy coordination.

**To/from Mountain Time (MT):** MT is 2 hours behind ET in most cases. Note that Arizona (excluding Navajo Nation) does not observe Daylight Saving Time, which can create a 3-hour gap during DST periods.

**International calls to 201:** From the UK, dial 00 + 1 + 201 + 7-digit number. From Australia, dial 0011 + 1 + 201 + 7-digit number. On mobile globally: +1-201-XXX-XXXX.

Because 201 shares the Eastern Time Zone with Manhattan, businesses managing cross-Hudson client relationships face no time zone adjustment. A 201 number integrates seamlessly with a New York City business workflow.

## Is a 201 Area Code Call a Scam?

The 201 prefix is entirely legitimate — it serves real residents and businesses across Hudson and Bergen counties. However, the code's high recognition in one of the most economically active corridors of the East Coast makes it an attractive target for **caller ID spoofing**, where fraudsters falsify the displayed number to appear local.

Two scam patterns are particularly well-documented in the 201 region:

### OSHA Compliance Scams

This is one of the most distinctive and widely reported scam types targeting New Jersey businesses. Callers using names like "Kevin Adams" or "Raymond" leave urgent voicemails claiming the business is out of compliance with state or federal OSHA safety requirements. They spoof local numbers on caller ID but direct callback to a different number. Once engaged, they attempt to sell unnecessary safety training materials or consultation services that carry no regulatory standing. Real OSHA enforcement contact comes in writing, not by unsolicited phone call.

### Utility Company Switching Fraud

This scam specifically targets NJ consumers and businesses by impersonating PSE&G (Public Service Electric and Gas) or Elizabethtown Gas. Callers claim they need to "review your supply charges" and ask the recipient to have their bill ready. Once the caller has the account number, they switch the customer's energy supplier to a third-party provider with significantly higher rates — without the customer's informed consent.

### Neighbor Spoofing

Scammers also use neighbor spoofing — displaying a 201 number that shares your own prefix — to increase the likelihood of an answer. If a call from an unfamiliar local number leaves no voicemail and matches your prefix closely, it is likely spoofed.

### How to Stay Protected

- Hang up on any caller claiming urgent OSHA or compliance violations without a prior written notice.
- Do not provide account numbers to anyone claiming to be your utility provider over an unsolicited call.
- Use a reverse phone lookup to check unfamiliar numbers before returning calls.
- Businesses operating legitimate 201 lines benefit from **STIR/SHAKEN-compliant** VoIP providers — this framework verifies caller ID data at the carrier level and reduces the effectiveness of spoofing.
