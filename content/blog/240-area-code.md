# 240 Area Code: Maryland's Location, Cities, and How to Get a Number

Picture a customer in Rockville scanning their phone before deciding whether to pick up. A number that starts with 240 reads as a neighbor. An unfamiliar one reads as a sales pitch. That small difference — local versus unknown — is exactly why the **240 area code** still matters for anyone doing business in Maryland.

This guide walks through where the **240 area code** sits on the map, which cities and counties it reaches, the time zone it follows, why it shares territory with 301 and 227, and the simplest way to claim a 240 number of your own.

## Where Is the 240 Area Code?

The **240 area code** serves western and central Maryland. It blankets Maryland's slice of the Greater Washington, D.C. metro, stretches west into the Appalachian communities near the state's panhandle, and dips south into the counties below the capital.

Geographically, 240 is not a small footprint. Its coverage includes all of Allegany, Charles, Garrett, Montgomery, Prince George's, St. Mary's, and Washington counties, most of Frederick County, western Howard County, and slivers of Carroll and Anne Arundel counties. One detail trips people up: despite some online listings tagging it as a "Baltimore" code, **area code 240** does **not** cover the city of Baltimore. Baltimore proper uses 410, 443, and 667.

## Cities Covered by the 240 Area Code

The **240 area code** reaches a long list of well-known Maryland communities. Among the most populous and recognizable are:

- **Montgomery County:** Rockville, Bethesda, Silver Spring, Gaithersburg, Germantown, Potomac, Montgomery Village

- **Prince George's County:** College Park, Greenbelt, Landover

That spread means a single 240 number can read as "local" to a tech worker in Bethesda, a university student in College Park, and a small-business owner in Hagerstown alike.

## A Short History of the 240 Area Code

The **240 area code** went live on June 1, 1997. It was not carved out of new territory — it was layered directly on top of the existing 301 region as an *overlay*. Maryland's suburbs were running out of available 301 numbers, and rather than splitting the area in two and forcing half the population to change their numbers, regulators introduced 240 to cover the identical map.

That is why longtime residents often keep a 301 number while newer lines get 240, even on the same street. The same pressure has continued. A third code, 227, was added to the very same overlay to keep the supply of available numbers flowing. So today 301, **area code 240**, and 227 all blanket the exact same geography in western and central Maryland.

One practical consequence: **ten-digit dialing is mandatory**. Because three codes share one footprint, even a call across the street requires the full area code plus the seven-digit number.

Neighboring Delaware operates under a single statewide code — the [302 area code](/blog/302-area-code) — a contrasting approach to number planning where one code covers the entire state, with no overlay needed.

## Is a 240 Call Safe? Scams and Cell Phone Myths

Two questions come up constantly, so let's clear them up.

**Is 240 a scam code?** No. This is a legitimate, geographically assigned Maryland code. Like any area code, it can be spoofed by bad actors, but the prefix itself is real and tied to a genuine region. A 240 call is no more "automatically" suspicious than a call from any other U.S. area code — judge the caller, not the prefix.

**Is 240 only for cell phones?** No. The 240 prefix is assigned across residential landlines, mobile phones, businesses, universities, hospitals, and VoIP services. It is a general-purpose geographic code, not a wireless-only one.

## What Time Zone Does 240 Use?

The entire 240 region sits in the Eastern Time Zone. During the winter months that means Eastern Standard Time (UTC−5), and from spring through fall it shifts to Eastern Daylight Time (UTC−4). For anyone scheduling calls, support hours, or outbound campaigns, treat every 240 number as Eastern Time — the same clock as New York, Atlanta, and Washington, D.C.
